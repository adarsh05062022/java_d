GRDM reproduction · downstream utility audit

Fidelity said both databases were fine. Only one of them is usable.

GRDM’s synthetic databases reproduce the paper’s fidelity scores on both California and Berka. Training XGBoost on them tells a different story: California synthetic data is a near drop-in replacement for the real thing, while Berka synthetic data retains barely a quarter of the signal. Diagnosing why traces the gap to two root causes — conditioning depth and gradient starvation on small tables — and combining fixes for both recovers roughly half of what was lost.

Does mixing help?

Not measurably — but it does no harm either, which is the useful finding when synthetic data is added for privacy or volume rather than accuracy. Every task stays within 0.03 of its real-only baseline at every ratio.

Why Berka collapses

Two different failure modes, and only one of them is visible in the per-column distributions that the fidelity metrics measure.

Loan default: the marginal itself breaks

Class B — a loan that finished unpaid — has 24 rows in the real training split and between 1 and 10 in every generated one. The default rate falls from 0.112 to about 0.073. Berka scored 96.89 Column Shapes on these same tables.

Card ownership: the marginal is fine, the dependency is gone

The share of dispositions holding a card is reproduced almost exactly — 0.168 real against 0.160–0.167 synthetic — and the task still falls from 0.874 to 0.602 AUROC. What is lost is the cross-table relationship between account behaviour and card ownership, not the rate. Matching per-column distributions is not sufficient for downstream utility.

Two leaks found while building this

The first feature sets produced real-data AUROC of 1.0000 and 0.9805. Those were ceilings measuring the schema, not the model. Berka’s label encoders name the cause exactly.

trans.k_symbol = SANKC. UROK — sanction interest

Charged only once an account is already in debt, which is what a defaulted loan is. With a negative minimum balance it separated the classes perfectly. Fixed by restricting transaction aggregates to trans_date < loan_date, making this a genuine prediction at origination. That leaves a median of 30 prior transactions per loan and drops the sanction signal from 77 accounts to 8. Real AUROC became 0.872.

trans.operation = VYBER KARTOU — card withdrawal

Cannot occur unless the disposition holds a card, so it determines the label outright. Cards are issued a median of 348 days after the account opens with only 51% inside the first year, so no time window separates cause from effect here. The deterministic giveaway is dropped and the task is scored as a cross-table dependency probe rather than a forecast. Real AUROC became 0.874.

Why Berka fails

GRDM’s GNN has n_hops layers, so while a row is generated it sees its own noisy features plus its direct neighbours’ raw columns — nothing further. Scoring XGBoost on real data restricted to exactly that window reveals the ceiling the generator works against.

Essentially all Berka signal lives two hops away — but widening the window has a cost

A loan conditions on its account’s two columns; the account’s ~235 transactions — which carry the signal — are two hops out. California’s individual ← household is exactly one hop, which is why the same configuration works there. The measured synthetic scores sit on these ceilings: baseline 0.60 on card against an own-row ceiling of 0.604; n_hops=2 reaches 0.67–0.69 against the 1-hop ceiling of 0.671. The effective window sits one hop behind the nominal depth, because a neighbour’s features are themselves still noisy during generation.

Widening the window is not free: it costs loan default 0.065 AUROC (3-seed mean), most likely because the wider fan-out further dilutes the seed share of small tables that are already gradient-starved. See the fix section below.

Depth and gradient balance are coupled — neither alone is the full answer

S2 (deeper conditioning) nearly doubles retained signal on card ownership (27.3% → 45.1%, 3-seed mean) but actively hurts loan default (23.6% → 6.8%), the opposite of what the diagnosis predicted. S3 (balanced seed sampling) is the strongest single fix for loan default (23.6% → 41.5%) and the only fix that improves fidelity over baseline rather than trading it away.

S5 combines both and wins on card ownership outright (48.5%, best of any fix) while recovering most of S2’s loan-default loss (34.0% vs S2’s 6.8%). The likely reason: 2-hop conditioning widens every table’s effective batch, further diluting loan’s already-thin seed share; balanced sampling directly offsets that.

S1 is the most informative negative result: it restores every categorical marginal exactly — loan.status class B from 1–10 rows back to 24, default rate 0.073 → 0.113 — and moves downstream AUROC by less than 0.005 either way. The minority-class collapse is the most visible symptom and is not what breaks utility. S4 (one-hot categoricals) is harmful everywhere — it broke California (synthetic-only R² −4.97, worse than predicting the mean) and was dropped after one Berka seed.
