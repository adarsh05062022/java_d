Follow-up to AMEX Report #15 · Relational Synthetic Data

The Berka Downstream Utility Investigation

GRDM reproduces the paper’s fidelity numbers, then loses three-quarters of the predictive signal on Berka. This is why, what we tried, what the mechanism turned out to be, and where it stands.

Date  2026-09-30 Prepared by  Adarsh Dubey Supervisor  Dr. Parimala Kancharla Model  GRDM (NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2505.16527)

The one-paragraph version

On California (2 tables, 1 hop) GRDM synthetic data keeps 95–99% of the downstream signal. On Berka (8 tables, depth 4) it keeps ~24% — while scoring the same 96–99% on every fidelity metric. The signal Berka needs lives in cross-table aggregates (how many transactions an account has, and their size), and GRDM’s architecture caps the neighbour sample so the true fan-out is never even an input to the model.

We ran 15 fix configurations, each at 1 or 3 generator seeds. Three separate single-seed “wins” evaporated once we added seeds, and multinomial categorical diffusion — the last of the original program — finished negative (card 33.2%, loan −2.2%, below chance). For most of this study the best 3-seed config was date-reparameterization + explicit degree features at 32% / 35%.

A follow-up study (§04) then tested the mechanism directly and sharpened it. The failure is not that synthetic data encodes different relationships — a synthetic-trained model scores near chance on synthetic data itself. The loss is confined to child-aggregate features, which keep 11–13% of their predictive signal while own-row / 1-hop features keep 60–99%.

That diagnosis then paid off. Materialising six child rollups as ordinary columns on the parent tables — the six-column test (§06) — takes card ownership to 48.7% retained and loan to 30.8% at 3 seeds, on the published architecture with no flags changed and no extra compute. It is the best 3-seed card number of the program and the best combined result, beating reparam + degree by 14 points on card at equal loan. The fix is in the input representation, not the network — which is the strongest evidence yet that the bottleneck is what GRDM is allowed to see, not what it can learn.

01

Why the downstream utility was low

Two mechanisms, and neither is visible in the fidelity metrics.

a. The predictive signal lives in aggregates GRDM never sees

For the Berka tasks, what actually predicts “loan default” or “card ownership” is information you get by rolling a child table up into the parent row — the count of an account’s transactions and orders, and the mean / spread of transaction amounts. Our aggregate diagnostic (“A6”) isolated the failure:

  • Two count columns alone (trans_n, order_n) carry loan-default AUROC from 0.67 to 0.82, against a 0.89 ceiling.
  • That count / fan-out family survives generation at only ~7% of its real correlation with the label — and it stays pinned at ~7% in the baseline, S2, S3 and S5. Every other feature family improves under the fixes; the counts never move.
  • Mechanism: GRDM’s --num_neighbors hands the GNN a fixed number of child rows (16 after the first hop). The real number of transactions is therefore absent from the input, not merely noisy — no amount of depth or gradient tuning can reconstruct it. Feeding degree in explicitly (S10) touches it; materialising the rollups in the data (agg, §06) moves it much further.

b. Two different failure modes — only one shows up in the marginals

Berka — loan default

The marginal itself breaks. The rare “default” outcome (class B: 24 real rows) collapses to 1–10 synthetic rows; default rate 0.112 → ~0.073. A classifier trained on that has almost no positive examples to learn from.

Berka — card ownership

The marginal is kept almost exactly (rate 0.168 → 0.16) and AUROC still falls 0.87 → 0.60. What is lost is purely the cross-table dependency between account behaviour and card ownership — not the rate.

The card result is the clean proof: matching per-column distributions is not sufficient for downstream utility, which is exactly the claim a column-shape metric cannot test.

The paradox in one line

Berka scores 96.9 on Column Shapes and 91.4 on 1-Hop fidelity — a few points below California — yet retains an order of magnitude less downstream signal. On the S2 fix, fidelity and utility move in opposite directions.

02

What measures we took

A systematic fix program — one change per experiment, every experiment scored on the same fixed leakage-free test set, at 1 or 3 generator seeds × multiple XGBoost repeats (3 California, 5 Berka).

Why the seed count matters

Berka loan default has only ~19–24 positive cases in the test set. A single generator seed on that task is noise. Everything reported as a headline below is 3 seeds; 1-seed rows are marked provisional and treated as pilots only.

04

Pinning the mechanism — five follow-up experiments

Section 01 asserted that the missing signal lives in cross-table aggregates. That was inferred from the fix program. These five experiments were run to test it directly, on the leakage-free berka_split generator, 3 GRDM seeds × 5 XGBoost seeds, reusing the existing feature builders and hyperparameters unchanged.

Headline — the hypothesis survived, and sharpened

The failure is not that synthetic data learned different relationships. A model trained on synthetic Berka scores near chance on synthetic test data itself — there is almost nothing there to learn. And the loss is confined to child-aggregate features: own-row and 1-hop features keep 60–99% of their predictive signal, cross-table rollups keep 11–13%.

Why this rules out “different relationships”

If synthetic data had learned different but internally consistent rules, synth→synth would be high and synth→real low. Instead synth→synth is at chance. And on card, a real-trained model scores 0.5963 on synthetic rows while a synthetic-trained one scores 0.5941 — a model that demonstrably knows the real rule does no better on synthetic data than one trained on synthetic noise. The structure is missing, not different.

The number that closes the loop

disp_type — a direct parent–child link — keeps 102% of its signal (real 0.595 → synth 0.597, KS 0.005) and is the synthetic model’s top feature by gain. Honest limits of these five experiments

Card’s control group is imperfect. year (0.611→0.520) and frequency also degrade, so the card contrast is 10.7% vs 40.9%, not a clean 10% vs 100%. Loan’s control is the clean one at 99.4%.

Fan-out count is not what carries card signal. trans_n (real AUROC 0.534) and order_n (0.512) barely separate the classes in real data — balance aggregates do. This qualifies section 01’s emphasis on counts: the A6 diagnostic found counts carry loan-default signal, but on card ownership it is the balance and amount rollups that matter.

Loan’s named aggregates are all at chance in real data (0.43–0.49) because its trans rollups are cut off at trans_date < loan_date. The loan arm above uses the code-share rollups that actually carry signal.

What a single loan seed would have told us

Had this study run seed 0 only, we would have reported synthetic→real loan transfer at 0.629 — 44% of the real model’s above-chance AUROC, roughly double the true 3-seed figure of 19.7% — and concluded that loan-default dependencies partially survive. They do not.

Card ownership shows no such fragility: its widest cross-domain cell range is 0.020 and every aggregate is dead in all three seeds. Card is where these conclusions rest; loan corroborates in direction, not in magnitude.

05

What the results signify

  • It is a structural limitation, not a tuning bug. The quantities that carry the Berka signal are all child aggregates — record fan-out on loan default, transaction-balance rollups on card ownership (trans_balance_mean alone is worth 0.838 AUROC). GRDM caps the neighbour sample, so the true fan-out is not an input at all; the balance rollups fare no better. Two levers moved it: adding degree features as an explicit input (S10), and — far more effectively — materialising the rollups themselves as parent columns (agg). Both work by putting the missing statistic in front of the model rather than asking it to infer one.
  • The synthetic data is empty, not wrong. §04’s cross-domain matrix is the decisive evidence: a synthetic-trained model scores 0.594 on synthetic test data, barely above chance. There is no alternative relationship to transfer — only an absence. Any framing that treats synthetic data as “biased” or “shifted” misdescribes what is happening. And the absence is fillable: under agg the same synth→synth cell rises to 0.649 on card and 0.709 on loan, so the synthetic database now genuinely carries learnable structure it did not carry before.
  • The damage has a precise address. Own-row and 1-hop features survive generation (60–99% of signal); child aggregates do not (11–13%), and for the strongest of them the synthetic AUROC’s 95% CI covers 0.5 in every seed. trans_balance_last keeps its marginal to KS 0.045 and loses its relationship to the label entirely — the exact failure a column-shape metric cannot see.
  • Fidelity ≠ utility for deep schemas. A relational synthesiser can score 96–99% on the published metrics and be near-useless for a multi-hop predictive task. Any AMEX-style evaluation needs a downstream-utility gate, not just distribution matching.
  • More depth is not the answer. 1 → 2 → 3 hops trades one task for the other and costs fidelity; it never recovers the count signal.
  • The two tasks pull in opposite directions. Loan wants shallow + class-balanced (a marginal problem); card wants degree features / depth (a dependency problem). No single published config wins both.
  • Best honest state today: the six-column agg variant — 48.7% card / 30.8% loan, 3-seed-confirmed, on the published architecture. It supersedes reparam + degree features (34.5% / 31.7%) by 14 points on card at equal loan. Card is now within sight of “usable”; loan is not, and still trails S3’s 41.5%. No config wins both.
  • Process: every result must be multi-seed. Three separate apparent wins evaporated once seeds were added — and §04 found the same fragility inside the diagnostic itself: seed 0 alone would have put loan transfer at 44% of real signal instead of the true 19.7%. The fragility is specific to loan default; card ownership is stable to within 0.02 AUROC across seeds, which is why the mechanism conclusions rest on card.
06

What to try next

Primary — completed 2026-09-30

Feed the relational aggregates to GRDM explicitly. This was the top recommendation of the previous revision, and §04 turned it into a testable causal claim: if the missing utility is exactly the child-aggregate signal, then exposing those aggregates as parent columns should recover some of it.

Six rollups — trans_n, trans_balance_mean, trans_balance_last, trans_amount_mean, order_n, order_amount_sum — are computed on split_train only and added to the account and disp tables. Architecture, training procedure, split and downstream protocol are unchanged, so the only difference is the added relational information. The downstream feature builders select their columns explicitly and never read the injected ones, so the evaluation still recomputes every aggregate from the generated child tables — no shortcut.

Result — the hypothesis held. All three seeds are in. Synthetic→real AUROC on card rises from the §04 baseline of 0.608 to 0.688, and on loan from 0.571 to 0.593. Expressed as retained signal: card 29.2% → 48.7%, loan 19.7% → 30.8%.

Three caveats before this is quoted. (1) No fidelity numbers exist for agg — the SDV step aborts on the undeclared agg_* columns, and every card-improving variant except S12 previously cost fidelity, so this column is load-bearing. (2) The six rollups were chosen using downstream knowledge — they are the features §05 found carrying real signal. This is not circular at evaluation time (the feature builders select their columns explicitly and recompute every aggregate from the generated child tables), but on an unseen schema you would not know which six to pick; AMEX needs a schema-general rule, e.g. count + mean/sum/last per numeric child column. (3) Most of the gap is still open — 0.688 against a real ceiling of 0.869 is partial recovery. Denormalising aggregates helps; it does not fix relational generation.

Closed since the last revision

Multinomial categorical diffusion is not the answer. It finished at 3 seeds: card 33.2% retained (above the 27.3% baseline), loan −2.2% — synthetic-only AUROC 0.492, i.e. below chance, per-seed 0.535 / 0.488 / 0.452. It fixed the minority-class collapse it was built for without recovering loan utility, which is consistent with §04: loan’s problem is not only the marginal, it is the missing cross-table dependency.

Incomplete — single-table ceiling check

The flatten baseline did not survive training. The idea was a ceiling: train an ordinary single-table diffusion model on the fully flattened join, where no relational structure has to be learned at all, and read off how much of the Berka loss is relational rather than generative. It is the control that would tell us whether 48.7% is close to or far from what is achievable.

On card, 2 of 3 seeds trained; seed 0 produced NaNs during sampling. Those two seeds are interesting — the flattened model retains 36–71% of the aggregate features’ signal against GRDM’s 3–5% — but 2 seeds with a third that diverged is not reportable, and this study’s own rule is that loan needs 3. On loan it failed outright: training loss went to NaN by step 10,000 and stayed there for the remaining 20,000 steps, then sampling raised FoundNANsError and killed the script before any loan result was written. About 3.2 GPU-hours went into a diverged model.

The loan encoding is the likely culprit: 511 rows against d_in=198 with 45 missingness flags. downstream/results/berka_split__flatten_baseline.json is a stale all-NaN write from the earlier smoke run — the two usable card seeds exist only in logs/flatten_full.log. Needs a numerical fix (gradient clipping / loss scaling), not a re-run.

Then, in order:

  • Restore the fidelity column for agg — declare the agg_* columns in the SDV metadata so the evaluation step stops aborting. Until that exists we cannot claim agg keeps the 96–99% fidelity, and every previous card gain except S12 cost fidelity. Cheapest open item, and it gates the headline.
  • Generalise the six columns into a rule — count + mean/sum/last per numeric child column, applied mechanically rather than hand-picked from the diagnostic. This is the only version of the result that transfers to AMEX, and it also tests whether the gain came from the specific six or from denormalisation as such.
  • Stack agg with S3 — agg carries card, S3 carries loan (41.5%), and they touch different parts of the pipeline (input columns vs. seed sampling), so they should compose. This is the most plausible route to a config that clears 45% on both.
  • Fix the flatten baseline’s numerics — without that ceiling we do not know whether 48.7% is near the achievable limit or far from it.
